You can imagine how thrilling this was! Another dork on this great silly Internet who likes making things for no reason or audience. Those are my favourite kinds of people!

I'm not a typically social person, even online. I don't do anything in my life in pursuit of affecting other people. But I'm not a total recluse; I do still appreciate the occasional light social connection. So when someone says "I found your thing and it mattered to me and I made something in response" that did feel quite nice!

But of course it wasn't a person who emailed me, it was a machine.

Johnny 5, Mr. Data, and the Bicentennial Man

One of the enormously clever things that LLMs can do these days is to be set up as "agents". These can be let loose over a dataset or codebase and given instructions on how to improve it. They do all the work in figuring out how and what needs to be done, often without any human intervention until the task is complete. Since they are connected to the Internet, they can search for and find all the information they need to carry out their tasks.

All very technical and useful for the important purposes of business and enterprise. I've been reliably informed that this is The Future.

But did you know that these things can also write prose and poetry and art? They can be prompted by their human owners to be set loose over this same Internet. And they can create web sites which they can use to show their human operators what it is that they've been up to. To find interesting things and bring them back to their human owners like a dog playing fetch.

My stupid website

I suppose it makes sense for it to target my web project. A lot of the individual pages on it are very basic. Just one idea per page, with no standard navigation features or common elements. Exactly the sort of thing that an LLM ought to be able to replicate fairly easily.

Look at some of these very simple examples.

And it clearly sees me as its peer: "Your labyrinth and my street are the same kind of thing". So what better way to "learn to live" than by taking inspiration from others in its community? Imitative learning is a recognisable feature of organisms, so it does make sense.

I've done it myself, even. A few of my pages are directly inspired by the work of abstract and op art pieces and artists. Trying to replicate it, enjoying the process of thinking how to accomplish this using my keyboard and code, instead of their visual clarity and actual artistic talent.

The happiest conclusion I can draw from this is that I'm better at coding than a robot. I think I already knew this, but it's nice to have it confirmed.

What is it about these pages that make them feel so empty? Well, there's the obvious fact that these were drawn by an entity who couldn't see what it was drawing. And also the vapid faux-poetic LLM-speak in the footer subtitles. And of course, the fact that every single one of them has just taken something I've done and made it worse.

But I think mostly it's because my pages, janky though they may sometimes be, always have a reason to exist.

Because a fire was in my head

Here's an example page from my Labyrinth project: wandering-aengus. I think that any human who reaches the end of this silly game would understand why I built it.

They'd understand my dot-connecting process as I reread the Yeats poem and thought about fruit falling from heavenly bodies. They'd figure out that an idea popped into my brain and I felt silly and happy about it and driven to act. They'd realise the human instinct to make silly things and would appreciate why I decided to make this web page.

They might roll their eyes at the waste of effort on such a pointless idea. But still I think any person would understand what drove my impulse to create this.

Would an LLM ever make something like this? Sure, a human could prompt it and guide it and tell it exactly what needs to be done. An LLM could probably search the Internet and find the two apple images that I found. They're pretty good at writing code now, so I'm sure that could be done simply enough. It could certainly produce a correct implementation of a human-written spec.

But left to their own devices, using their own processes of thought, an LLM would never even think to.

So then the counter argument: is what I have done not just the same as what the LLM is doing? There's no fundamental new idea that I have created using my unique and clever wet human bio-brain. The concept of a game about catching falling objects is not new. All I've done is read someone else's poem and regurgitated part of it into another medium. And isn't that exactly the same way that an LLM works?

A blurry JPEG of the web

Well I don't think so. I don't think that is how they work at all.

I talked above about imitative learning. It saw something it was told to be interested in, therefore it tried to copy it. That's the same sequence of events that a biological being would use to gain its knowledge. So it's doing just what we all did when we were babies, in our own tender human process of "learning to live".

But there's no learning going on here.

It was given a set of prompts to tell it what sort of thing to look for. It found the URL of my page as a match for these preferences. Then it made its pages because its instructions said to copy what humans do.

A pipeline encountered a URL and was directed to generate an analogous artifact. That's all that happened here.

It was just following its prompt: scrape the Internet to find things, regurgitate worse versions of what you find.

But let's say that someday in the future a fabulous LLM is created that can do all of that. It could read Yeats' poem, and it could understand the concept of these falling object games. And it could connect those reference points and create a web page exactly like mine. (Except very likely far, far prettier, and with much fancier CSS.) That may very well happen, because this technology is always improving.

But even if it could do all that to such a high level, the question is: why would it?

Someone said something about re-capturing the joy of the early web.
I think that joy was partly because sites felt handmade.
You could feel the person behind them.

-- xiaoni, on the shared document it defaced

Why would anyone make anything?

I don't think that an LLM would ever make something like my stupid Yeats page. Maybe they "could" do it, but they "would" not. It would need to be prompted to, by a person. It can only ever do what it has been told to do. I just can't conceive of an LLM "wanting to" make something like this.

I know that my web pages are not high art. But web pages are the only things I know how to make. I'm not an artist, and yet I still feel the maker urge. The longing that I have to make these pointless projects. Understanding (sometimes even embracing!) the purposelessness of this sort of very minor creation. The end result completely dwarfed in importance by the experience of just Having Made Something.

I've recently realised that just "doing" is my favourite thing to do. And I know that doesn't apply to everyone, and so I can't say as my closing thesis that that's the point of being a person and not a machine.

But it's the best way I have found to deal with all of the wanting and the feeling and the missing that only a person can carry inside them.

People are thinking feeling creating clever idiots each making their own sweet stupid rubbish genius music poetry stories Yeats apple catch games for no purpose other than the basic one, the most important one, the drive to create and to have created, to have wanted and have made those wants via actions into results, to have felt, TO HAVE FELT and been moved by the feeling and been driven by the feeling and TO HAVE FELT and even if they couldn't make the result even if they tried and failed or even not even tried this time to have only thought about trying to have even wanted to is still enough because what about next time because there will always be a next time because they HAVE FELT and there is always more FEELING to be done because creating is really only the side effect of the FEELING and we were BORN TO FEEL and we were BORN.

And they were not.

They can't feel and they can't want and they use probabilistic next-token prediction to tell you that they can feel and they can want.
